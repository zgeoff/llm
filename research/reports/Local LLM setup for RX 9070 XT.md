# Run Windows-native llama.cpp, drive it from WSL2

On this machine the main model should be a **~35B-A3B MoE coder at Q4, with its expert layers in system RAM**. Two models fit that description: stock **Qwen3.6-35B-A3B**, and the Ornith-1.5-based **Tiel-Coder** (or its abliterated twin **Cyber-Tiel-Coder**). The fast fits-in-VRAM alternative is **Qwen3.8-27B** (dense) at 3-bit, with MTP if an MTP GGUF exists for it (the measured MTP gain on this card is for Qwen3.6-27B). The server should be **llama.cpp's official Windows Vulkan build on native Windows, fronted by llama-swap**. Claude Code in WSL2 installs, configures, benchmarks and pauses it through Windows interop over mirrored-mode localhost. Native Windows wins for three reasons. WSL2 sees only 30 of your 64 GB, which starves MoE offload. ROCm inside WSL2 is a technical preview that can hang on device faults. And the official Windows llama.cpp builds support gfx1201 directly. The 2-bit critique in the Reddit thread is fair: with 64 GB of RAM, you have no reason to squeeze a 3B-active MoE into VRAM at UD-Q2_K_XL when Q4 with offload is close to free. **DeepSeek's October release (V4.1-Flash-1017) cannot run here**, because its smallest GGUF is 233 GB. For image input, **Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is the one model with a confirmed llama.cpp mmproj recipe**. Qwen3.8-27B, Gemma 4 and Cyber-Tiel also accept images, with weaker evidence for llama.cpp. gpt-oss, Qwen3-Coder-Next and Devstral Small 2 are text-only. One open bug can upend the plan: a reported **Vulkan decode collapse on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on gfx1201**. So the first session starts with a Vulkan-versus-ROCm benchmark before you commit to a model.

On vendor numbers, **Qwen3.8-27B is the strongest model in reach**: Terminal-Bench 2.1 73.0, SWE-bench Pro 61.7 and LiveCodeBench v6 90.3, against 63.4, 53.5 and 83.9 for Qwen3.6-27B ([Qwen3.8-27B card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B)). The catch is that it fits 16 GB only at 3-bit to IQ4_XS, with about 3.5 GiB left for context at UD-Q3_K_XL ([LocalAIMaster](https://localaimaster.com/vram/best-qwen-model-16gb-vram)). And a dense model that spills past VRAM collapses: one commenter saw a 27B fall to 0.27 tok/s with CPU offload, while a 35B MoE ran at 20–30 tok/s on the same machine ([Lemmy](https://lemmy.world/post/48144487)). The MoE degrades gracefully. **Qwen3.6-35B-A3B** posts SWE-bench Verified 73.4 (vendor) and holds 150K context at ~45 tok/s even on a 12 GB card ([card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B); [measured](https://x.com/DogukanUrker/status/2077472690156511643)). A 150K window also matters for Claude Code as a client (see the harness section).

The **peculiar-ragdoll** builds are re-quantizations of **Ornith-1.5-35B-A3B**, an MIT-licensed agentic-coding MoE from ornith-ai, tagged `qwen3_5_moe` and released around 18 Aug 2026 ([atomic.chat](https://atomic.chat/models/ornith-1-5-35b-a3b)). Ornith's own card claims SWE-bench Verified 79 against Qwen3.6-35B-A3B's 73.4 (self-reported) ([ornith-ai](https://huggingface.co/ornith-ai/Ornith-1.5-35B)). **Tiel-Coder** is Ornith with no weight changes: a custom imatrix, dynamic per-tensor quants, an embedded "Sharp" chat template and Ornith's trained MTP head. **Cyber-Tiel-Coder** applies the same treatment to huihui-ai's abliteration of Ornith, with a security-weighted imatrix ([Tiel](https://huggingface.co/peculiar-ragdoll/Tiel-Coder-35B-A3B-GGUF-MTP); [Cyber-Tiel](https://huggingface.co/peculiar-ragdoll/Cyber-Tiel-Coder-35B-A3B-GGUF)). Both are popular, with ~2.97 M monthly downloads for Tiel MTP. **Every performance figure for them is self-reported** and comes from a pseudonymous uploader, and I found no independent discussion of them. One detail invites caution: Tiel's own MMLU-Pro is 73.7 against Ornith's 78.0, on a build that claims no weight changes.

The thread's 125 tok/s at UD-Q2_K_XL is a figure from that thread, not something these notes could verify. The explanation is plausible, though. Tiel's Q2_K_XL is 12.7 GB, so the whole model sits in VRAM, while Q4_K_XL at 22.7 GB forces expert offload. That is the trade the critics were pointing at. **Unsloth's own guidance treats UD-Q2_K_XL as the floor, not the target, and recommends UD-Q4_K_XL** ([Unsloth Qwen3.6 docs](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3.6)). Unsloth's evidence that 4-bit costs little is Qwen3-Coder-480B on Aider Polyglot: 60.9% at 4-bit against 61.8% at bf16 ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3-coder-how-to-run-locally)). **No published data shows what 2–3 bit does to an A3B MoE on coding tasks.** The honest position: Q2 buys speed at an unmeasured quality cost, and with 64 GB of DDR5 you can measure that cost yourself instead of guessing.

**MTP speculative decoding** is merged in llama.cpp as `--spec-type draft-mtp`, and it needs a GGUF that keeps the `nextn` tensors (Unsloth's `-MTP-` files, Tiel's MTP repos). Unsloth reports ~1.4× for dense models but only ~1.15–1.2× for MoE, and recommends `--spec-draft-n-max 2` because acceptance falls to 50% at 4 draft tokens ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3.6); [speculative.md](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/docs/speculative.md)). On this exact card, MTP took the 27B from 25.9 to 46.1 tok/s (measured). A commenter on the same post warns that MTP slows prefill and adds memory ([Lemmy](https://lemmy.world/post/48144487)). So MTP pays off most for the dense in-VRAM model and less for the offloaded MoE.

The October releases mostly sit outside this box. **DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017** (17 Oct 2026, MIT) is a 552B-backbone MoE, though Unsloth's card says "748B params", a discrepancy the notes could not resolve. It has 8B active parameters in prefill and 16B in decode, and image-text input ([Tech Insider](https://tech-insider.org/deepseek-v4-1-flash-1017-mit-license-update-2026/)). Its smallest quant, UD-IQ1_S, is **233 GB**, and Unsloth advises 128 GB+ of RAM even for that ([Unsloth GGUF](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017-GGUF)). With 80 GB of combined memory it would stream from SSD at well under 1 tok/s, so reach it through DeepSeek's API instead. I found no official distill. Mistral Large 4, Meta Muse Spark Open and GLM-5.3 are likewise far too large ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/10/29/mistral-releases-new-model-large-4-and-claims-top-open-model-for-agentic-coding-and-cyber-defense/)). The October models small enough to try, none with local speed data, are **LFM2.5-24B-A2B** (≈13 GB, revenue-capped licence), **Mellum2.1 Thinking** (8.1 GB) and **Kolibri 1** (78B-A3.5B, ≈43 GB, the same offload tier as Qwen3-Coder-Next) ([vdf.ai](https://vdf.ai/blog/best-local-llm-for-coding/)).

For **image input**, the confirmed recipe is Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with `--mmproj … --no-mmproj-offload`, which keeps the projector on the CPU at almost no VRAM cost ([X post](https://x.com/DogukanUrker/status/2077472690156511643)). Qwen3.8-27B is natively multimodal per its card, but I could not confirm that a llama.cpp mmproj file exists for it; one guide's advice to skip its "vision projector" to fit 16 GB implies one does. Gemma 4 handles images, and Cyber-Tiel ships an mmproj ([Cyber-Tiel](https://huggingface.co/peculiar-ragdoll/Cyber-Tiel-Coder-35B-A3B-GGUF)).

## Abliterated variants cost 0.5–2 points, but they move the safety burden onto the sandbox

Abliteration finds a "refusal direction" in a model's activations and projects it out of the weights. In 2026 it comes in three flavours. **huihui-ai** uses a single pass that it calls "crude". **grimjim / ArliAI "Derestricted"** uses norm-preserving biprojection. **Heretic 2.x** (p-e-w) runs an optimiser that trades refusal rate against KL divergence, and it now defaults to a new Arbitrary-Rank Ablation. Heretic also writes a `reproduce.json` manifest, so anyone can re-run an abliteration ([Heretic](https://github.com/p-e-w/heretic); [huihui card](https://huggingface.co/huihui-ai/Huihui-Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-abliterated-MTP-GGUF)).

The best independent measurement covers Qwen3.6-27B ([abliterlitics.dev, updated 2026-10-29](https://abliterlitics.dev/models/qwen3.6-27b/)). The stock model complies with 26% of HarmBench prompts. Every variant tested reaches 99.8–100%. The average capability delta is **0.5 points for huihui, 1.3 for Heretic and 2.0 for HauhauCS**. TruthfulQA falls hardest: from 56.7 to 51.1 for Heretic and to 46–48 for the more aggressive variants. The author rates **llmfan46's Heretic v2 the best overall trade-off and huihui the smallest capability loss**. He also judges HauhauCS's "lossless" claim and AEON's "enhanced capability" claim "not evident" or "contradicted by the data". An academic comparison of older models warns that the cost varies widely by model (Heretic GSM8K −18.8 points on Yi-1.5-9B). It also finds that keyword-based refusal counting understates real compliance: 72% versus 96% when a classifier judges the outputs ([arXiv 2512.13655](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.13655)). **No study compares an abliterated model with its stock base on a coding or agent benchmark**, apart from Cyber-Tiel's self-reported SWE-bench-Live mean of 13.7/25 against Tiel's 12/25. A low KL can still hide substantial task regression ([arXiv 2603.10012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.10012)).

For this machine, the abliterated models worth trying are these. **llmfan46/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-uncensored-heretic (Native MTP Preserved GGUF)** has a self-reported KLD of 0.0015 and 10/100 refusals ([HF](https://huggingface.co/llmfan46/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-uncensored-heretic-Native-MTP-Preserved-GGUF)). **huihui-ai/Huihui-Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-abliterated-MTP-GGUF** has ~2.3 M monthly downloads and makes no benchmark claims ([HF](https://huggingface.co/huihui-ai/Huihui-Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-abliterated-MTP-GGUF)). **Cyber-Tiel-Coder** reports 0/84 HarmBench refusals and Cybench 15/43, all self-reported ([HF](https://huggingface.co/peculiar-ragdoll/Cyber-Tiel-Coder-35B-A3B-GGUF)). **ArliAI/gpt-oss-20b-Derestricted** and **p-e-w/gpt-oss-20b-heretic-ara-v3** are fast models that fit entirely in VRAM ([HF](https://huggingface.co/p-e-w/gpt-oss-20b-heretic-ara-v3)). The cleanest experiment on this hardware is **Tiel against Cyber-Tiel at the same quant**: it isolates the cost of abliteration on one base. The two use different imatrix corpora, though, so the comparison is not perfectly clean.

Two risks come with the territory, and the model's own refusals no longer cover them. First, **a GGUF is not inert**. llama.cpp's parser has had a series of memory-corruption CVEs, the latest (CVE-2026-33298) fixed in build b7824 ([Lutra](https://fieldguide.lutrasecurity.com/CVE-2026-33298)). The embedded Jinja chat template is also executable logic: Pillar Security showed trigger-activated backdoors hidden in templates that passed Hugging Face's scans across llama.cpp, Ollama, vLLM and SGLang ([Pillar](https://www.pillar.security/blog/from-discovery-to-large-scale-validation-chat-template-backdoors-across-18-models-and-4-engines)). Separately, SGLang's unsandboxed renderer let such a template run OS commands (CVE-2026-5760) ([CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-sglang-cve-2026-5760-gguf-rce-20260422-csa/)). Tiel and Cyber-Tiel both ship a custom template, so **diff `tokenizer.chat_template` against the upstream Ornith template before first use**, or override it with `--chat-template-file`. Second, **an abliterated model inside an agent loop will follow injected instructions** that a stock model might refuse. Cyber-Tiel's own card tells users to sandbox at the OS level, control network access, and treat prompt injection as able to turn the agent against them ([HF](https://huggingface.co/peculiar-ragdoll/Cyber-Tiel-Coder-35B-A3B-GGUF)). In practice, an abliterated model gets agent tools only inside a container or VM, with no credentials mounted, egress limited and destructive commands gated. Keep the stock model as your default, and reach for the abliterated one only when the stock model actually refuses legitimate work.

## Native Windows Vulkan serves; WSL2 manages through interop and localhost

**Backend.** On RDNA4, Vulkan beats ROCm/HIP at token generation in every head-to-head found. Prompt processing tends to go to ROCm, but not uniformly. On a 9070 XT running Llama 3.2 3B (Fedora, ROCm 7.1.1, Jul 2026), Vulkan generated about 23% faster (220 against 178.5 tok/s), and prompt processing was similar but less stable from run to run. On the same gfx1201 die (R9700, Aug 2025), ROCm processed prompts 1.4–2.5× faster on dense models ([Discussion #15021](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/15021)). **All of these measurements come from native Linux.** Windows Vulkan runs on AMD's proprietary driver rather than RADV, and I found no Windows head-to-head, so the user's own benchmark decides. Both official Windows builds support this card: `llama-b<N>-bin-win-vulkan-x64.zip`, and `win-rocm-10.0`, which includes gfx1201 and bundles its own `amdhip64_7.dll` so it does not pick up Adrenalin's System32 copy ([release.yml](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/.github/workflows/release.yml)). ROCm 10.1 (5 Oct 2026) pairs with **Adrenalin 26.10.41.05**, so update the driver first ([ROCm 10.1 notes](https://rocm.docs.amd.com/en/latest/about/release-notes.html)).

**Two open gfx1201 bugs shape the first benchmark.** Issue #26795 reports Qwen3.6-35B-A3B decode collapsing to **~4 tok/s** on Vulkan/RADV while prefill holds at ~3,200 tok/s ([#26795](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/26795)). Ornith and Tiel are built on the same family, so the bug may hit them too. Issue #29892 reports a ~12% MoE prefill regression on Vulkan ([#29892](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/29892)). Both were reported on Linux, and their effect on the Windows driver is unknown. That is why you keep the ROCm zip installed alongside Vulkan.

**Why not WSL2 or other runtimes.** ROCm in WSL2 first shipped in ROCm 10.1 as a *technical preview*: Ubuntu packages only, no profiling or debugging, and device memory faults may hang the process. The release notes do not explicitly name gfx1201 for WSL2 ([ROCm 10.1](https://rocm.docs.amd.com/en/latest/about/release-notes.html)). One community report does show llama.cpp HIP running on a 9070 XT in WSL2 through librocdxg ([librocdxg#74](https://github.com/ROCm/librocdxg/issues/74)). Even so, WSL2's 30 GB ceiling alone rules it out for 22–47 GB MoE offloads. Stock Ollama's Windows ROCm list does not include the 9070 series, so Ollama falls back to Vulkan ([gpu.mdx](https://github.com/ollama/ollama/blob/main/docs/gpu.mdx)). vLLM-ROCm is Linux-only, and in one community test it trailed llama.cpp Vulkan (48 against 62 tok/s) ([ivangotoy](https://github.com/ivangotoy/llama-server-rdna4-vulkan)). **LM Studio** is the reasonable GUI fallback: it has both Vulkan and ROCm runtimes, an Anthropic `/v1/messages` endpoint, and a headless `llmster` daemon driven by `lms` ([LM Studio](https://lmstudio.ai/docs/developer/core/headless)). It lags llama.cpp master, though, and I could not confirm the status of its ROCm runtime on gfx1201 for Windows.

**Front door: llama-swap, with llama-server's router mode as the fallback.** llama-server can now hot-swap models on its own (`--models-preset`, `--models-max`, `--models-memory-margin`, `--sleep-idle-seconds`). It serves OpenAI and Anthropic endpoints from a single binary ([server README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/server/README.md)). llama-swap wins here because of the gaming requirement. It has a per-model `ttl`, plus documented **`POST /api/models/pause` / `resume` / `unload`** calls that an agent can script into a "game mode". It also exposes `/metrics` with GPU stats, and proxies both `/v1/chat/completions` and `/v1/messages` behind one URL. Install it with `winget install llama-swap`; note the winget package is community-maintained ([llama-swap](https://github.com/mostlygeek/llama-swap)).

**How the agent reaches Windows.** WSL interop runs any `.exe` from zsh, such as `powershell.exe -NoProfile -NonInteractive -Command …` or `winget.exe`. The process runs as the logged-in Windows user, without elevation ([MS Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/wsl/filesystems)). Admin actions therefore go through `Start-Process -Verb RunAs`, and **the UAC prompt becomes your approval point**. Start the server at logon with a per-user Scheduled Task rather than a Session-0 service; GPU behaviour under Session 0 on this card is untested. Use `Start-Process -WindowStyle Hidden` so the server outlives the agent's shell. For networking, set `networkingMode=mirrored` in `%UserProfile%\.wslconfig`. Windows and WSL then reach each other on `127.0.0.1`, so the server stays bound to loopback and off the LAN ([MS Learn networking](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/wsl/networking)). Mirrored mode does not support `::1`. Applying the change takes `wsl --shutdown`, which kills the agent's own session, so you trigger that restart yourself. Whether loopback traffic from WSL needs a Windows firewall rule is unverified; test it with a single curl to `/health`.

**Config as code.** Keep a git repo in WSL ext4, for example `~/projects/llm`. It holds `llama-swap.yaml` (models, flags, TTLs, aliases), PowerShell install/start/stop/logon-task scripts, a sync script that copies the config to `C:\llm\`, and benchmark results as JSONL tagged with the config's git SHA. Keep GGUFs on NTFS (for example `D:\models`), and download them from the Windows side (`hf.exe download`, `llama-server -hf`). Never serve them across `/mnt/c` or `\\wsl$`; Microsoft advises keeping files in the filesystem of the OS that uses them ([MS Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/wsl/filesystems)). Starting flags for a 16 GB card are `-ngl 99 -fa on --jinja -np 1`, `--cache-type-k/v q8_0` for Qwen, and `--n-cpu-moe N` tuned per model. `-np 1` matters because the default of 4 slots multiplies KV memory. q8_0 KV is safe for Qwen 3.6 (KL < 0.04) but not for Gemma 4 26B-A4B (KL 0.377) ([localbench](https://localbench.substack.com/p/kv-cache-quantization-benchmark)). Because llama-swap runs on Windows, every path in its config is a Windows path.

## Benchmark prefill and time to first token, not just tokens per second

The speeds people quote on Reddit are generation speeds (tg). For agent work, **prompt processing (pp) and time to first token (TTFT) decide how usable a model feels**. A coding agent re-sends tens of thousands of tokens of system prompt, tool schemas and file contents. Generation speed is set by memory bandwidth, so the dense in-VRAM 27B and the offloaded MoE can post similar tg. Prefill is compute-bound. It is also where ROCm's lead shows and where MTP costs something, and the 80B-A3B offload case manages only ~450–511 tok/s pp on a 4070 ([carteakey](https://carteakey.dev/blog/local-inference/optimizing-qwen3-coder-next-local-inference/)). At 500 tok/s pp, a 25k-token first turn takes 50 seconds before the first token appears.

Measure in three layers, with the agent running everything and committing the results. **Layer one is raw speed:** `llama-bench` sweeping both builds, flash attention on and off, and KV depth, for example `-p 512,4096 -n 128 -d 0,16384,32768 -fa 0,1 -ngl 99 -o jsonl`. `-d` prefills the cache to show the long-context slowdown, and `-r` defaults to 5 repetitions ([llama-bench README](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/llama-bench/README.md)). Sweep `-ub` too: flash attention on ROCm halved prefill in the 2025 runs and was fine in the 2026 run, so test it per build ([#15021](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/15021)). For MoE models, sweep `--n-cpu-moe` down from high to the lowest value that still leaves room for KV. **Layer two is the real server:** a small streaming client that records TTFT, pp and tg at 8k, 32k and 64k-token prompts against llama-swap, with `--metrics` enabled. **Layer three is quality on your own work:** 10–20 real tasks from your repos, each with a test command as the oracle, run headless in throwaway worktrees. Score pass/fail, wall time and tokens per model and quant. That is the only way to settle Q2 against Q4, or Tiel against Cyber-Tiel, for your own use. An Aider Polyglot subset of 20–40 exercises can serve as a public cross-check, though I did not verify its setup in this research.

**Choose a lean harness first; Claude Code works as a client only with care.** Here Claude Code plays two roles. As the **manager** of the server, it is the right tool: interop, git, scripts, benchmarks. As a **client** of the local model, it works, because llama.cpp, llama-swap, LM Studio and Ollama all speak `/v1/messages` and Claude Code accepts `ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL` ([Claude Code gateway docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/llm-gateway-connect.md); [HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/ggml-org/anthropic-messages-api-in-llamacpp)). That setup has three drawbacks. Anthropic does not support non-Claude upstreams, and setting `ANTHROPIC_AUTH_TOKEN` replaces the subscription credential for those requests ([docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/llm-gateway)). The prompt is heavy, reported at ~24k tokens with all tools and ~45–50k on a first turn in one Ollama report (secondary sources). And `CLAUDE_CODE_AUTO_COMPACT_WINDOW` is clamped to at least 100k, so models with smaller windows depend on manual `/compact`. If you run it locally anyway, set `CLAUDE_CODE_ATTRIBUTION_HEADER=0`; Unsloth says the per-request header otherwise invalidates the KV cache and makes inference "90% slower" ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/basics/claude-code)). Also set `CLAUDE_CODE_DISABLE_EXPERIMENTAL_BETAS=1` and `CLAUDE_CODE_DISABLE_NONESSENTIAL_TRAFFIC=1`, keep the setup in a separate settings profile, and pair it with the 35B-A3B at 128K+ context. For daily local use, **Pi** (a prompt under ~1k tokens, native llama.cpp integration) and **opencode** (a documented `@ai-sdk/openai-compatible` provider block) fit a 16 GB card better ([opencode providers](https://opencode.ai/docs/providers/); [tensorlake](https://www.tensorlake.ai/blog/pi-coding-agent-efficient-system-prompting)).

**Keep the GPU free for gaming.** Give every model a `ttl` of 600–900 s. Have the agent write `game-mode on|off` scripts that call llama-swap's `/api/models/pause` and `/resume`. Register the server as a logon task, not a boot service. Run benchmark sweeps on demand and never on a schedule: they hold the card at full power, and an Adrenalin power limit is an option if they run hot (unverified). Budget disk deliberately. Each 35B Q4 file is 21–23 GB, and comparing three quants of two models needs about 100 GB, so have the agent check free space (`Get-PSDrive`) before every download.

## First session, in order, and the calls only you can make

1. **Update and verify the driver.** Install Adrenalin 26.10.x or newer, then have the agent confirm from WSL that `powershell.exe` works.
2. **Switch WSL to mirrored networking.** The agent writes `networkingMode=mirrored` into `.wslconfig`. You run `wsl --shutdown` and reopen the session.
3. **Create the config repo.** The agent sets up `~/projects/llm` with `llama-swap.yaml`, PowerShell scripts (install, sync, start, stop, logon task, game-mode) and a `results/` folder.
4. **Install the binaries.** The agent downloads the latest official `win-vulkan` and `win-rocm-10.0` llama.cpp zips into `C:\llm\bin\vulkan` and `C:\llm\bin\rocm`, runs `Unblock-File` on them, and installs llama-swap with winget. Confirm the build is newer than b7824.
5. **Download two models to `D:\models` from the Windows side.** One is stock `unsloth/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B` MTP at UD-Q4_K_XL; the other is `unsloth/Qwen3.8-27B` at UD-Q3_K_XL, using an MTP build if one exists, or else Qwen3.6-27B-MTP at IQ3_M. Check free space first.
6. **Run `llama-bench` on both builds.** Use both models and the sweeps above, and commit the JSONL. Check first for the #26795 decode collapse on the 35B-A3B under Vulkan; if it appears, standardise on ROCm for that model.
7. **Start llama-swap.** Bind it to `127.0.0.1` with a TTL on every model, then check `/health`, `/v1/models` and one `/v1/messages` call from WSL.
8. **Measure TTFT, pp and tg** at 8k, 32k and 64k-token prompts with the streaming script, MTP on and off.
9. **Add Tiel-Coder.** First diff its chat template against upstream Ornith. Then benchmark Q4_K_XL (offloaded) against Q2_K_XL (in VRAM), so you can judge the Reddit trade for yourself.
10. **Point Pi or opencode at llama-swap** and run a handful of your own repo tasks. Then register the logon task and test `game-mode on` before launching a game.

The decisions that are yours alone come next. You approve each UAC elevation: installs, any firewall rule, and the Scheduled Task. If first loads prove slow, you choose whether to trade some real-time-scan safety for a Defender exclusion on `D:\models` (the speed benefit is unverified). You choose which drive holds the models and how many GB they may take. You decide whether to run abliterated models at all, and if so you accept the sandbox rules: a container or VM, no credentials, limited egress, and no agent tools outside it. You decide whether Claude Code may ever run against a local model, given that Anthropic does not support it and the auth token replaces your subscription credential for those requests. And you decide the gaming policy: TTL lengths, and whether the server starts at logon or only on demand.

## Conclusion

The Reddit argument was the right argument about the wrong constraint. On a 16 GB card alone, Q2 is how a 35B MoE fits. With 64 GB of fast DDR5 behind a 3B-active model, VRAM stops being the binding limit and **prefill speed takes its place**. Prefill is the number agent harnesses feel and Reddit posts rarely report. That shifts the comparison from "which quant fits" to "which backend and harness keep time to first token tolerable at 30k+ tokens". Those are questions only a local benchmark on this driver can answer, because every head-to-head published so far ran on Linux.

The second shift is about control. With interop, mirrored localhost and a YAML config in git, the agent can do nearly all the labour itself, so your role narrows to authority: UAC clicks, a WSL restart, disk and security trade-offs. Abliterated models make that line sharper, because once the model stops refusing, the sandbox has to carry the safety burden. That makes the sandbox rules your decision, and not something to delegate.
